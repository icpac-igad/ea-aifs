Given the scope—**AIFS-ENS at 0.25° extending to about 800 hours**—I would not begin with a large ontology of individual meteorological variables. Begin with a **small grouped physical DAG**, where each node represents a process and is estimated from only a few AIFS-ENS fields.

The main limitation is important:

> AIFS-ENS can represent the atmospheric forcing and perhaps antecedent land-state proxies, but it cannot independently resolve catchment saturation or river response at 25 km.

Therefore, your first operational endpoint should probably be **catchment-scale hydroclimatic surplus or deficit potential**, rather than actual river discharge or impacts.

## Recommended starting DAG

```text
Large-scale circulation
          │
          ▼
Moisture supply and transport
          │
          ▼
Rainfall-generation environment
          │
          ▼
Precipitation forcing
          │
          ▼
Antecedent catchment wetness
          │
          ▼
Catchment water-balance pressure
          │
     ┌────┴─────┐
     ▼          ▼
Water-surplus  Water-deficit
potential      potential
```

For flood-oriented reasoning, the pathway is:

```text
Moisture supply
      +
Rainfall-generation environment
      ↓
Heavy or persistent precipitation
      +
Antecedent wetness
      ↓
Catchment water-surplus potential
```

For drought-oriented reasoning:

```text
Weak moisture supply
      +
Persistent rainfall suppression
      +
Atmospheric evaporative demand
      ↓
Accumulating catchment water-deficit potential
```

This avoids trying to represent every atmospheric mechanism separately.

# Four process groups to start with

## 1. Large-scale circulation regime

This node describes whether the circulation is favourable for transporting and concentrating moisture over the region.

### Minimum variables

* Mean sea-level pressure: `msl`
* Geopotential height, preferably 500 hPa: `z500`
* Zonal and meridional wind:

  * `u850`, `v850`
  * optionally `u700`, `v700`

### What the node represents

```text
Pressure and geopotential pattern
             ↓
Direction, strength and persistence
of regional atmospheric circulation
```

Do not put `msl`, `z500`, `u850`, and `v850` directly as four independent parents of rainfall. They are highly related. Derive one grouped state such as:

```text
Circulation regime
├── unfavourable
├── neutral
├── moisture-convergent
└── strongly moisture-convergent
```

For East Africa, this can capture whether the flow is bringing moisture from the Indian Ocean, Congo Basin, Red Sea or another source region.

---

## 2. Moisture supply and transport

This is probably the **most important synoptic node** for your first experiment.

### Minimum variables

* Total column water vapour: `tcwv`
* Specific humidity at 850 or 700 hPa: `q850`, `q700`
* Winds at the corresponding levels:

  * `u850`, `v850`
  * `u700`, `v700`

From these, derive vertically integrated or layer moisture transport.

Conceptually:

[
\mathbf{IVT}
============

\frac{1}{g}
\int q\mathbf{V},dp
]

If enough pressure-level humidity and wind fields are available, calculate IVT directly. Otherwise, use a lower-tropospheric moisture-flux approximation:

[
\mathbf{MFT}_{850}
==================

q_{850}(u_{850},v_{850})
]

### Derived evidence

Use spatially meaningful indicators rather than raw grid-cell values:

* Moisture-flux magnitude
* Moisture-flux direction
* Moisture-flux convergence
* Duration of anomalous moisture transport
* Ensemble probability of exceeding a local percentile

The ontology node could be:

```text
Moisture supply and transport
├── deficient
├── normal
├── enhanced
└── extreme/persistent
```

For both flood and drought, this node is more informative than precipitation alone because it describes the physical supply of atmospheric water.

---

## 3. Rainfall-generation environment

I would use this phrase instead of only **atmospheric instability**, because East African heavy rainfall is not always dominated by local convective instability. It can arise from moisture convergence, organised convection, orographic lifting and large-scale ascent.

### Candidate variables

Start with:

* CAPE: `cape`
* Temperature at 850 and 500 hPa: `t850`, `t500`
* Relative or specific humidity at 700 hPa: `q700` or `r700`
* Vertical velocity at 500 or 700 hPa: `w500` or `w700`, where available
* Geopotential and wind fields for diagnosing troughs and convergence

### Grouped interpretation

```text
Rainfall-generation environment
├── suppressed
├── weakly supportive
├── supportive
└── strongly supportive
```

This node should not mean only “high CAPE.” For example:

```text
Strong moisture convergence + moderate instability
                    ↓
          Supportive environment
```

and

```text
High CAPE + dry middle atmosphere + no convergence
                    ↓
       Only weakly supportive environment
```

That distinction prevents CAPE from being treated as a direct predictor of flooding.

---

## 4. Precipitation forcing

This is the atmospheric output that directly enters the catchment.

### Core variable

* Total precipitation: `tp`

### Derived features

Do not use only accumulated rainfall at a single lead time. Derive:

* 6-hour or 24-hour precipitation
* 3-day accumulation
* 7-day accumulation
* Maximum rolling 24-hour rainfall
* Number of wet forecast steps
* Consecutive wet days
* Ensemble exceedance probability
* Forecast persistence between successive runs

For example:

```text
Precipitation forcing
├── below normal
├── normal
├── heavy episodic
└── heavy persistent
```

The distinction between **episodic** and **persistent** is important:

* A short extreme burst may cause flash flooding.
* Moderate but persistent rainfall may saturate a large catchment.
* Repeated rainfall episodes can produce delayed river flooding.

---

# Catchment wetness cannot come from the current forecast alone

This is the first major blocking wall.

A 15–33-day AIFS-ENS forecast tells you what may happen next, but catchment saturation depends strongly on what happened **before initialization**.

You therefore need an antecedent-state node:

```text
Antecedent catchment wetness
```

This should ideally combine:

* Observed rainfall over the preceding 7–30 days
* Soil-moisture analysis or satellite soil moisture
* Previous runoff or streamflow
* Existing reservoir, lake or wetland conditions where relevant

AIFS-ENS forecast precipitation alone should not be used to infer the initial catchment wetness.

A simple first version could use:

[
AWI_t
=====

\sum_{i=1}^{n} P_{t-i}k^i
]

where `AWI` is an antecedent wetness index and older rainfall receives progressively less weight.

The node could be:

```text
Antecedent wetness
├── very dry
├── dry
├── normal
├── wet
└── saturated
```

## Combined catchment pressure node

Then construct:

```text
Precipitation forcing ─────────┐
                              ├──► Catchment water-balance pressure
Antecedent catchment wetness ──┘
```

This target node might have five states:

```text
Strong deficit
Mild deficit
Balanced
Elevated surplus
Extreme surplus
```

That single ordered node can support both drought and flood reasoning.

# Smallest viable variable set

I would start with approximately **eight physical fields**, not thirty:

| Process                 | Initial fields                              |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Circulation             | `msl`, `z500`                               |
| Moisture transport      | `q850`, `u850`, `v850`, `tcwv`              |
| Rainfall environment    | `cape` or `w500`                            |
| Atmospheric water input | `tp`                                        |
| Antecedent state        | External observed rainfall or soil moisture |

If pressure-level coverage permits proper IVT calculation, add:

* `q1000`, `q925`, `q850`, `q700`, `q500`
* Corresponding `u` and `v`

However, I would treat this as the **second iteration**. A lower-level moisture-flux proxy is adequate for testing the ontology.

# The BN should contain grouped states, not every field

A sensible initial BN would be:

```text

        │
        ▼
Moisture transport ──────────────┐
        │                        │
        ▼                        ▼
Rainfall-generation ───► Precipitation forcing
                                  │
Antecedent wetness ───────────────┤
                                  ▼
                   Catchment water-balance pressure
                             ┌────┴────┐
                             ▼         ▼
                      Surplus alert  Deficit alert
```

A slightly more physically explicit version is:

```text
Circulation regime ──► Moisture transport
                              │
                              ├──► Rainfall environment
                              │            │
                              └────────────┤
                                           ▼
                                Precipitation forcing
                                           │
Antecedent wetness ────────────────────────┤
                                           ▼
                             Catchment surplus/deficit
```

## Avoid this structure

```text
u850 ─┐
v850 ─┤
q850 ─┤
tcwv ─┤
cape ─┼──► Heavy rainfall
w500 ─┤
z500 ─┤
msl  ─┘
```

This is difficult to interpret and likely to double-count correlated meteorological information.

Instead:

```text
u850 + v850 + q850 + tcwv
                 ↓
       Moisture-transport index
                 ↓
          BN evidence node
```

The diagnostic calculation occurs **outside the BN**. The BN reasons with the resulting process state and its uncertainty.

# Ensemble information should remain explicit

Because this is AIFS-ENS, the evidence should not be reduced immediately to the ensemble mean.

For each grouped node, retain:

* Probability of each state
* Ensemble spread
* Agreement among members
* Persistence across lead windows
* Change from the previous forecast cycle

For example:

```text
P(enhanced moisture transport) = 0.72
P(extreme moisture transport)  = 0.18
Ensemble agreement             = moderate
Trend from previous cycle      = increasing
```

This becomes soft evidence in the BN rather than a hard category.

# What I would implement first

The first prototype should test only this chain:

```text
Lower-tropospheric moisture transport
                ↓
Rainfall-generation environment
                ↓
Ensemble precipitation forcing
                +
Antecedent wetness
                ↓
Catchment water-surplus probability
```

Use four diagnostic products:

1. 850-hPa moisture flux or IVT.
2. Moisture-flux convergence over and upstream of the catchment.
3. Rolling precipitation accumulations and ensemble exceedance probabilities.
4. Antecedent rainfall or soil-moisture percentile.

For drought, use the same structure but evaluate longer windows:

```text
Deficient moisture transport
             +
Suppressed precipitation probability
             +
Dry antecedent state
             ↓
Increasing water-deficit probability
```

At the 800-hour horizon, the BN should progressively change its interpretation:

* **0–120 hours:** event-scale surplus and heavy-rainfall reasoning.
* **120–360 hours:** persistent wet/dry regime reasoning.
* **360–800 hours:** broad water-balance tendency, not catchment-specific flood prediction.

AIFS ENS v2 is currently ECMWF’s operational ensemble AI system, following its May 12, 2026 upgrade; ECMWF describes the AIFS products as global 0.25° forecast data, but the exact parameter subset depends on the chosen product stream and access route. ([ECMWF][1])

The key starting principle is therefore:

> **Use only a handful of fields, but organise them into physically meaningful process nodes: circulation, moisture transport, rainfall-generation environment, precipitation forcing and antecedent catchment state.**

The most important first node is probably **moisture transport**, while the most important non-AIFS input is **antecedent catchment wetness**. Without the latter, the BN can estimate heavy-rainfall potential, but not credible catchment water-surplus risk.

## Recommended order

### First: atmospheric river, Somali jet and Turkana jet

These are closer to the actual weather-producing mechanism and can be diagnosed from the same AIFS-ENS fields already used for moisture transport and circulation.

They should usually be treated as **derived circulation regimes**, not as completely separate independent parents.

For example:

```text
Large-scale circulation
        │
        ├── Somali-jet state
        ├── Turkana-jet state
        └── Atmospheric-river state
                  │
                  ▼
        Moisture transport/convergence
                  │
                  ▼
          Precipitation forcing
```

However, this structure can still double-count information because atmospheric rivers and low-level jets are identified from wind, humidity and IVT—the same variables already informing the moisture-transport node.

A safer structure is:

```text
Somali/Turkana jet configuration ──► Moisture transport
Atmospheric-river configuration ───► Moisture transport
                                             │
                                             ▼
                                  Rainfall-generation environment
```

Or even more simply:

```text
Moisture-transport regime
├── ordinary transport
├── Somali-jet enhanced
├── Turkana-channelled
├── atmospheric-river-like
└── weak/deficient transport
```

This avoids treating strongly related diagnostics as independent evidence.

## ENSO and IOD come later

ENSO and IOD are different. They are not immediate weather mechanisms over a catchment. They influence the **background probability of circulation and rainfall regimes**.

So they belong upstream as prior-conditioning nodes:

```text
ENSO ─────┐
          ├──► Seasonal background state
IOD ──────┘              │
                         ▼
              Circulation-regime probability
                         │
                         ▼
                 Moisture transport
```

They should not normally point directly to flood or drought risk:

```text
ENSO ──X──► Flood risk
IOD  ──X──► Drought risk
```

That would be too coarse and could imply false causality.

Instead:

```text
ENSO/IOD
   ↓
Probability of particular circulation regimes
   ↓
Moisture supply and rainfall persistence
   ↓
Catchment surplus or deficit tendency
```

## Why ENSO and IOD should not be first

At short lead times, the actual AIFS ensemble circulation is more informative than a broad climate index.

For example, even during an El Niño or positive IOD period:

* a specific catchment may remain dry,
* the expected circulation teleconnection may not materialise,
* the rainfall anomaly may shift geographically,
* local moisture transport may oppose the seasonal expectation.

Therefore, ENSO and IOD should modify the prior, while AIFS-ENS provides the current evidence.

In Bayesian terms:

[
P(\text{wet circulation}\mid \text{ENSO, IOD})
]

provides a prior, which is then updated by:

[
P(\text{AIFS circulation and moisture evidence})
]

Add:

These change the prior likelihood of the Stage 2 regimes.

## Atmospheric river needs careful treatment

For East Africa, I would initially use the term:

> **organised moisture-transport corridor**

rather than immediately classifying every strong IVT event as an atmospheric river.

Classical atmospheric-river thresholds and geometry were developed mainly for extratropical regions. In tropical East Africa, strong elongated moisture transport may occur through monsoon flow, low-level jets, tropical disturbances and Indian Ocean inflow without fitting a standard atmospheric-river definition.

So the initial node could be:

```text
Moisture-corridor state
├── absent
├── weak
├── organised
└── extreme and persistent
```

Later, after event validation, you can decide which cases qualify as atmospheric rivers.

## Somali jet and Turkana jet should remain distinct

They serve different spatial and physical roles.

The **Somali jet** may influence large-scale moisture transport from the Indian Ocean and monsoon circulation.

The **Turkana jet** is strongly channelled by regional topography and can affect low-level transport and rainfall distribution around northern Kenya, South Sudan, Ethiopia and Uganda.

They should therefore not simply be merged into one generic “jet” variable. But they may both influence the same grouped node:

```text
Somali jet state ─────┐
                      ├──► Regional moisture-transport regime
Turkana jet state ────┘
```

## My strongest recommendation

Start with the physical process node:

> **Regional moisture-transport regime**

Then use Somali jet, Turkana jet and atmospheric-river diagnostics to explain or classify that regime.

Add ENSO and IOD only after that mechanism performs reasonably well. They should act mainly as **prior-conditioning variables**, not as direct predictors of catchment risk.

So the hierarchy is:

```text
ENSO / IOD
    ↓
Background likelihood

Somali jet / Turkana jet / moisture corridor
    ↓
Weather-producing mechanism

AIFS precipitation + antecedent wetness
    ↓
Immediate catchment evidence
```

That separation between **background climate**, **synoptic mechanism**, and **catchment evidence** will keep the BN interpretable and reduce double-counting.
